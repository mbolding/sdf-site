Auto Biology
2026-10-31

Dr. Ansel Whitcombe tagged his first Subaru on the morning of April
14, 2000, in the gravel lot below the Stowe Pinnacle trail, using a
pair of banding pliers and a numbered aluminum band meant for a
red-tailed hawk.

He had been, until that spring, an ornithologist. His grant on the
migratory habits of the bobolink had not been renewed, and he had
driven up to the trailhead mostly to sit in his truck and feel sorry
for himself. What he noticed instead, through a windshield fogged with
self-pity, was that the lot held eleven vehicles and seven of them
were Subarus. He came back the next weekend, and the weekend after
that. The composition of the lot was remarkably stable. The same green
Outback. The same maroon Legacy wagon with the cracked taillight. The
same Forester with a canoe strapped upside down on its roof like a
beetle carrying a leaf.

Site fidelity, he wrote in the field notebook he still kept in the
glove box out of habit. Possibly territorial.

The band went on the antenna of the green Outback. He crimped it
closed, wrote B-0007 in the notebook (the first six bands on the
string had gone to hawks, back when he still had a permit), and walked
back to his truck with his heart pounding in a way it had not pounded
since graduate school. The owner came down the trail two hours later,
a woman in her sixties with a wet dog, and drove away without noticing
a thing.

That was twenty-six years ago. As of this morning, Dr. Whitcombe has
banded 1,418 Subarus across four states and one Canadian province, and
recorded 3,960 recaptures. He is the author of nineteen papers on the
ecology of the genus, most of them in the Proceedings of the World
Congress of Auto Biology, of which he has been president since 2011.

The World Congress of Auto Biology has eleven members. Twelve, if you
count Dr. Fenwick, who has not paid dues since 1998 but still sends a
Christmas card signed "yours in the field."

It was founded in 1971 in Liège by a Belgian physician named Hélène
Marchand, who had spent a decade documenting the decline of the
Citroën 2CV in the Ardennes and wanted somewhere to present her
findings that would not laugh her out of the room. The Congress has
met every other year since, in whatever conference room its members
can afford. The 2024 meeting was held in the breakfast area of a
Hampton Inn in Kalamazoo, Michigan, between ten and eleven-thirty,
after the waffle station closed.

The membership is small but serious. Dr. Adebayo Oyelaran of Lagos has
spent thirty years on the Toyota Corolla, which he regards as the most
successful organism of the twentieth century, cockroaches included.
Mrs. June Pottinger, a retired veterinarian in Queensland, studies the
ute, and her monograph on its nesting behavior outside rural pubs is
considered definitive. Mr. Hiroshi Tanaka of Sapporo works on kei cars
and speaks about them with a tenderness that makes the other members
look at their shoes. There is a minivan man from Ohio whom nobody
quite trusts.

The Congress has its controversies. The 2019 meeting nearly ended the
organization when a motion to recognize electric vehicles as within
the scope of auto biology, rather than, as the opposition put it,
"appliances," passed five to four. Mr. Tanaka abstained, then wept.
Dr. Whitcombe, as president, cast no vote, but privately he sided with
the appliance faction, and he has never banded a Tesla, partly on
principle and partly because there is nowhere to put the band.

That last point is a sore one. The industry's shift to the stubby
shark-fin antenna did to auto biology what the draining of the
wetlands did to the wood stork. For three bad years Dr. Whitcombe's
recapture rates collapsed, until 2014, when he discovered that a size
9 band fit snugly around the base of the rear wiper arm. He presented
the finding at the Congress in Tucson and received a standing ovation,
which, with eight people in attendance, took some time to organize.

His findings, briefly:

Subarus exhibit strong fidelity to trailheads, climbing gyms, food
co-ops, and the parking lots of breweries in towns of fewer than
twenty thousand residents. They migrate seasonally, moving uphill
toward ski areas in winter and toward rivers in summer, often carrying
kayaks. They are long-lived. B-0007 crossed 300,000 miles in 2017, and
Dr. Whitcombe has documented individuals that survived deer strikes,
hail, and teenage drivers, sometimes in the same year.

Most significantly, he developed a method for aging a Subaru in the
field without consulting the owner. Expired ski-lift tickets
accumulate on the lower corner of the windshield in layers, one per
season, and national park stickers accrete on the rear window in a
predictable sequence. Counting them is not unlike counting tree rings.
He called the method windshield stratigraphy, and it is the thing he
is proudest of, and the thing nobody outside the Congress has ever
cited.

He does not concern himself with the owners. In his papers he refers
to them as the symbiont, in the sense that an oxpecker is a symbiont
of the rhinoceros. The dog is a second symbiont. There is nearly
always a dog.

Over twenty-six years, Dr. Whitcombe has supervised nine graduate
students. He could tell you where every one of them is, the way a
parent can tell you where each of their children lives, and with the
same undertone.

Dana went to veterinary school. He understands that, in a way. At
least she went toward animals.

Marcus became an actuary for an auto insurer in Hartford. "He still
studies vehicle mortality," Dr. Whitcombe will say, if asked. "Just
from the wrong end."

Lin went to California to work on self-driving cars, which Dr.
Whitcombe considers domestication, and which he discusses in the tone
other men reserve for divorce.

Tomás went back to birds. That one, at least, was honest.

Beckett became a poet. His second collection had a poem in it called
"Outback, Unbanded," and Dr. Whitcombe read it forty or fifty times,
trying to decide whether it was a tribute or an accusation.

The others drifted into ordinary jobs, as people do.

And then there was Priya Kaur, the best student he ever had, who could
age a Forester at forty yards by the fade on its roof rails alone. She
left the program eleven credits short of her doctorate and took a job
at a Subaru dealership in South Burlington. She is now the general
manager.

He has never said the words "captive breeding" to her directly. He has
said them to almost everyone else.

None of them followed him. He has made a kind of peace with that. He
is sixty-eight, and the Congress is eleven people and a Christmas
card, and when he goes the band numbers will simply stop, the way a
trail stops at a summit.

Every October, on the second Saturday, Dr. Whitcombe drives to the
gravel lot below the Stowe Pinnacle and waits for B-0007.

The green Outback has come back twenty-five years running. The woman
with the wet dog is long gone from the driver's seat; for the last
several years it has been driven by a stocky man in his forties whom
he assumes is her son, with a different dog, also wet. Dr. Whitcombe
does not speak to him. That would be interfering with the subject. He
only watches the car pull in, confirms the band on the wiper arm (he
moved it over in 2014, with great care), counts the new lift ticket,
and writes the date in the notebook. Then he drives home and has a
beer, and it is one of the best days of his year.

This year, it did not come.

He waited from seven in the morning until the light went orange behind
the ridge. He ate both sandwiches. He watched fourteen Subarus come
and go, three of them banded, all of them fine, none of them the one.
At five-thirty he wrote B-0007, not observed, and then sat looking at
the words for a long time.

A car with 300,000 miles does not go missing. It dies. He knew that.
He had written it in a paper.

He was not planning to stop at the dealership. It was on the way, that
was all, and he needed gas, and the gas station was across the road,
and he could see Priya through the showroom glass, standing at a desk
with her reading glasses pushed up into her hair.

She came out before he could leave. She had seen his truck. She always
saw his truck.

"You look terrible," she said.

"B-0007 didn't show."

She did not ask what that meant. She had spent four years in his lab.
She only nodded and said, "Come inside," and he followed her past the
new Crosstreks gleaming under the lights, past the coffee machine, and
into the service department, where she pulled a thick green binder off
a shelf and set it on the counter in front of him.

"It came in the second week of August," she said. "Head gasket. The
owner was going to scrap it, but his daughter was starting at Reed in
the fall and needed a car, so he paid for the repair and she drove it
to Portland."

"Oregon," Dr. Whitcombe said.

"Oregon."

He sat down on the stool by the counter. Three thousand miles. It was,
by an order of magnitude, the longest dispersal event he had ever
recorded. He would have to write it up.

Then he looked at the binder, and at the label on its spine, which
read, in Priya's small, square handwriting, PK series, recaptures,
2016 to present.

He opened it.

Every Subaru that had come through her service bay had a line.
Mileage. Tread depth. Lift tickets, counted and dated. Park stickers,
in order. Symbionts present, with dog breed where known. And in the
last column, a band number: PK, followed by four digits. She had never
used his prefix. That would have been presumptuous.

"They come back for oil changes," she said. "Every six thousand miles,
like clockwork. It's the best recapture site in New England. You were
doing it at trailheads, which, respectfully, is insane."

"How many?"

"Four thousand one hundred and twelve banded. A little over nineteen
thousand recaptures." She shrugged. "The techs put the bands on during
the tire rotation. They think it's a warranty thing."

He turned the pages slowly. Ten years of it. Ten years of her standing
at this counter, reading the windshield stratigraphy of every Outback
that rolled in, writing it down, telling no one.

"Why didn't you say anything?"

Priya was quiet for a moment. "Because you called it captive
breeding," she said. "At Dana's wedding. During your toast. Into the
microphone."

He had. He had forgotten she was in the room. He had thought, at the
time, that it was a very good line.

"I'm sorry," he said, and found that he meant it more than he had
meant anything in some time.

She pulled the binder back toward her, flipped to a tab near the end,
and turned it around again. There was B-0007, entered on the day it
came in. She had cross-referenced it with his number. Under Notes, she
had written: Oldest known individual. Banded by A. Whitcombe, Stowe,
2000. Released to the west in good condition.

The World Congress of Auto Biology will meet next year in the
conference room of a Best Western outside Missoula, Montana, which Dr.
Whitcombe chose because it is the closest thing to halfway between
South Burlington and Portland, Oregon. The program lists two
presentations from the United States. The first is his, titled "A
Twenty-Six-Year Longitudinal Study of Site Fidelity in Subaru outback,
With a Note on an Unprecedented Dispersal Event."

The second is Priya's.

At the end of the meeting, the president intends to stand down and
nominate a successor. He has not told her yet. He has decided, for
once, to let the subject come to him.
