The Salt Road

2026-11-08

On the seventeenth day out of Myrtlesee, Calvain Hest's pacer threw a chitin shoe and came up lame. He stood in the white road and considered the animal, which returned his look with the placid contempt that pacers reserve for men who have failed them.

Behind, the salt flats unrolled toward the horizon, lavender at this hour, faintly luminous. Ahead, perhaps another six days on foot, lay the bone-spires of Cobalt Mountain and the trading post at Yan. Calvain had two skins of water, a small wedge of pressed fig, a ceramic knife, and a pouch containing eleven pearls of the second water. He had no metal at all, of course, save the iron filings stitched into the hem of his cloak, which were not for trade but for the geomancer's compass he carried in his head, and which would be the first thing taken if he met the wrong company on the road.

He cut the pacer loose and watched it limp away toward a stand of glass-thorn, where it would either die or be eaten by something with more patience than himself. Then he shouldered his pack and began to walk.

By the second afternoon he had company. The man came up from a side track he had not noticed, a thin figure in a coat of pieced lizard-skin, walking with the long, loose stride of someone bred to distance. He carried a staff of fire-hardened cane and a wicker pannier on his back, and he fell in beside Calvain without invitation or greeting, as was the courtesy of the salt road, where to speak first was to claim a debt.

They walked perhaps two hours in silence before the stranger said, "You are from Myrtlesee."

"I was lately there."

"You walk like a man who expected to ride."

Calvain conceded this with a tilt of his head. "My pacer threw a shoe."

"Ah." The stranger considered. "There is a smith at the Three Wells, half a day off the road. He keeps spare beasts."

"I have nothing to trade for one."

"Pearls, perhaps."

Calvain said nothing. The stranger smiled without showing his teeth and they walked on.

That night they made a small fire of dried scuttle-vine in the lee of a salt pillar. The stranger produced from his pannier a packet of dried meat, which he shared without ceremony, and a small stoppered gourd of what proved to be a thin sour wine. Calvain contributed a portion of his fig. They ate, and watched the moons come up, three of them tonight, the small green one chasing the larger pair across a sky so wide it seemed to bend the horizon down at its edges.

"My name," the stranger said at last, "is Dyron Faurd. I am a collector of stories. I trade them at Yan and at the Floating Market when it comes through. A good story will buy a meal, sometimes a bed. A very good story will buy passage on a sand-barge."

"I have no stories."

"Every man on this road has a story. The only question is whether he knows he is carrying it."

Calvain looked at him across the small blue flame. "And if I had one, what would you give for it?"

"That depends on the story." Dyron Faurd refilled his cup with great care, as though the wine were precious, which perhaps to him it was. "I will tell you one of mine, by way of demonstration. Then you may decide."

Calvain inclined his head.

"In the country east of the Volturna," Dyron began, "there is a town called Ulm, where the people worship a god they have never seen. The god lives, they say, in a deep cleft in the basalt, and once a year the priests lower a basket of offerings into the cleft on a rope of woven hair. In the morning the basket is drawn up empty, and this is taken as proof of the god's appetite and goodwill.

"Now, there came to Ulm a young man named Sabin, who had been a student at the College at Avente before some misfortune drove him out, and who possessed therefore a corrupted education and a great curiosity. Sabin observed the ceremony of the basket, and he observed also that the cleft was deep but not unfathomable, and that a man with rope and patience might descend into it. So one night he did so."

Dyron paused to drink. Calvain waited.

"At the bottom of the cleft, Sabin found neither god nor monster, but a small clear pool, and beside the pool a passage leading away into the rock. He followed the passage, and it brought him out, after some hours of crawling, into a hidden valley on the far side of the basalt ridge. And in this valley lived perhaps two hundred people, ragged and thin, who fell upon him with cries of joy when they saw him, for he was the first new face they had seen in many years.

"They were the descendants, you understand, of the priests of Ulm, going back perhaps four generations. The first of them had discovered the passage, and had conceived the scheme of the basket, and had used the offerings to feed himself and his family in the hidden valley, where the soil was rich and the water sweet but the population, lacking new blood, had begun to wither. They begged Sabin to bring them others, to bring them women, to bring them tools and seed."

"And did he?"

"He told them he would. He climbed back up the rope, and he went to the elders of Ulm, and he told them everything he had seen."

"And?"

Dyron smiled his closed smile. "The elders thanked him gravely. They gave him a fine meal and a bed for the night. In the morning they bound him hand and foot and lowered him into the cleft in a basket. They had known about the valley for three generations. The basket goes down empty of food, sometimes, but it never goes down empty."

Calvain considered this for a long time. The green moon had passed behind the salt pillar and the fire had burned down to a knot of coals.

"That is a good story," he said at last.

"It is a serviceable story. I have better. But it is the one suited to this fire and this company." Dyron drew his coat around him. "Now you."

Calvain was silent so long that Dyron began to think he had given offense. Then Calvain said, "I was a clerk in the counting house of the Margrave of Myrtlesee. I had a wife, who was the Margrave's third cousin, and a son of four years. The Margrave kept a treasury of pearls, of which I was the under-keeper. One day an audit was ordered, and it was found that a certain number of pearls were missing. They were not missing because I had taken them. They were missing because the Margrave had taken them, to pay a gambling debt to a Brown Bersaglier captain, and had not troubled to amend the book. But a clerk cannot accuse a Margrave. A clerk can only run."

He drew the small pouch from his shirt and weighed it in his palm.

"These are eleven of the missing pearls. I took them on my way out, partly because I felt I was owed something for the loss of my life, and partly because I knew they would pursue me harder for theft than for flight, and a pursuit that has a clear cause is easier to anticipate than one driven by mere offended pride. My wife and son are at her mother's house in the Iron Hills, where the Margrave's reach does not extend. I am bound for Yan, and from Yan to the coast, and from the coast, I do not yet know."

He put the pouch away.

"That is my story. I do not know if it is good or serviceable. I have not told it before."

Dyron Faurd was quiet for a long while. Then he reached into his pannier and brought out a second packet, smaller than the first, and laid it on the salt between them.

"Dried apricot," he said. "From the orchards at Skene. I was saving it. Take it, please."

"You have not told me what my story is worth."

"I have not yet decided. I will tell you at Yan." He lay down with his pannier for a pillow and drew his coat over his face. After a moment he added, from beneath the coat, "There is no smith at the Three Wells. I told you that to see what you would say. I apologize. The road makes us cautious."

"I understand."

"Sleep. I will wake you at the second moon."

Calvain lay back on the warm salt and looked up at the sky. The stranger's breathing slowed and deepened. After a while Calvain reached out and took the packet of apricot and tucked it inside his shirt, against the pouch of pearls, where it would be warm.

Six days to Yan. Then the coast. Then he did not yet know.

But the road, he thought, was perhaps not so empty as he had feared.
