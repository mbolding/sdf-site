The Salt Road, Part Two

2026-11-08

Dyron Faurd woke him at the second moon, as promised, and they walked through the cool hours before dawn, speaking little. When the sun came up, a hard white disk pressing flat against the eastern edge of the world, they sheltered in the long shadow of a salt ridge and slept through the worst of the heat. So the days passed. On the fourth morning they came down from the salt flats into a country of low red hills, and on the fifth they struck the caravan track that runs from Port Abraxas to Yan, and began to meet other travelers.

The first was a woman leading two pacers laden with bolts of glass-cloth. She gave them good day and passed on. The second was a party of three Drusanes in their characteristic yellow cloaks, who eyed Calvain's boots with interest and Dyron Faurd's staff with more, and who also passed on, though more slowly. The third was a boy of perhaps eleven, alone, walking north with a small dog at his heel and no visible provision, who would not meet their eyes and who hurried past as though pursued. Calvain looked after him a long time.

"The road takes the young hard," Dyron said.

"He had no water."

"He had something. A boy does not walk that road on nothing. Perhaps a message. Perhaps a debt he is carrying for someone who could not carry it himself. It is not our affair."

Calvain said nothing, but that night when they camped he laid out a small measure of his own water in a shallow stone and left it at the edge of the fire's light, and in the morning it was gone, though whether to the boy or to some thirsty creeping thing of the red hills he could not say.

On the sixth day they came within sight of Yan.

The town lay in a bowl between two arms of Cobalt Mountain, and from the last rise of the caravan track one could see the whole of it: a disorder of white stucco and blue tile, a central square with a fountain that ran, astonishingly, with clear water, and at the eastern edge the low sheds and paddocks of the sand-barge terminus, where three of the great vessels lay at rest with their sails furled, looking from this distance like sleeping moths. Beyond the town the land fell away again into a pale shimmering distance, the Tchaam, the inland sea that was not a sea but a basin of fine white dust, across which the barges ran on their broad skids with the trade winds for motive power.

Dyron Faurd stopped on the rise and leaned on his staff.

"I will tell you now," he said, "what your story is worth."

Calvain waited.

"It is worth a warning, which I will give you freely, and a name, which I will also give you freely, and a small piece of advice, which you may take or not as suits you."

"Go on."

"The warning is this. There is a man in Yan called Jubal Ixo, who keeps a house of changers on the Street of the Four Dogs. He will give you a fair price for pearls, better than anyone else in the town, and he will not ask where they came from. But he will remember your face, and he has a standing arrangement with a certain factor of the Margrave of Myrtlesee, who visits Yan twice a year to collect debts and information. The factor is due in Yan within the month. Do not sell to Jubal Ixo."

Calvain felt the cold settle into his stomach, the particular cold he had carried since Myrtlesee, which he had begun in recent days to forget.

"The name," Dyron continued, "is Thessaly Morn. She keeps a smaller house on the Lane of the Tanners, and she pays perhaps a twelfth less than Jubal Ixo. But she is discreet, and she has no arrangements with anyone from Myrtlesee, because her sister was cheated by the Margrave's father in a matter of silk and she will take no coin from that quarter on principle. Tell her I sent you. She will know the name."

"And the advice?"

"Do not take the coast road from Yan. Take passage on a sand-barge across the Tchaam to Erze Damath. It costs more but it leaves no tracks. A man on foot on the coast road can be followed by a child with a good eye. A man who has crossed the Tchaam has, for practical purposes, vanished."

Calvain looked down at the town, at the three barges in their paddocks.

"Why are you telling me this?"

Dyron Faurd considered the question with apparent seriousness, as though it had not occurred to him before.

"Partly," he said, "because your story was better than you knew, and I am a man who pays his debts in his own coin. Partly because I have a daughter of my own, somewhere, whom I have not seen in nine years, and I like to imagine that if she were ever in flight, some stranger would do for her what I am doing for you. And partly, Calvain Hest, because I am not quite what I told you I was."

Calvain's hand moved, not quite to the ceramic knife at his belt, but in that direction. Dyron saw the motion and smiled his closed smile.

"Peace. I am a collector of stories, that much is true. But I am also, on occasion, a collector of persons. I was paid, three weeks ago at Port Abraxas, to watch the salt road for a clerk from Myrtlesee traveling alone, and to send word by semaphore from Yan if I found him. I accepted the commission because I was hungry and because I did not then know whether the clerk would prove to be a thief, a murderer, or a man who had merely stepped on the wrong toe."

"And now?"

"Now I know. And I find that I do not care to send the semaphore. I will tell my employer, when next I see him, that I walked the salt road for three weeks and found no one answering the description, which is nearly true, since the man I found is not quite the man described to me. The description I was given mentioned no wife and no son of four years. A man without dependents is a different quarry than a man with them. I do not hunt fathers. It is a small rule of mine, but I keep it."

He hefted his pannier and started down the rise toward Yan. After a few paces he looked back.

"Thessaly Morn, on the Lane of the Tanners. The barge to Erze Damath leaves on the third day of every week, at the first hour after dawn. Do not linger in Yan. Do not drink in the taverns near the terminus, where the barge-masters take their custom and where questions are asked of strangers as a matter of professional interest. Eat in the small places on the south side of the square, where the food is worse but the company does not look up when the door opens."

"Dyron."

"Yes."

"If you had decided otherwise, last night at the fire. If my story had been a different story."

Dyron Faurd considered the salt flats behind them, the long white road they had come down together.

"Then we would have reached Yan together also," he said, "and I would have sent the semaphore, and you would have been taken at the barge terminus or at the inn, whichever you came to first. You would not have known. I would have walked with you as a friend until the moment, and then I would have walked away, and in a year I would have remembered your face only as one of a number. It is what I do, Calvain Hest. Or it is what I did. Perhaps after today it is something slightly different. I have not yet decided."

He turned and walked on down toward the town. After a moment Calvain followed, keeping a little distance, as seemed proper for men whose business together was nearly concluded.

At the gate they parted. Dyron Faurd went east, toward the barge terminus, where he had his own arrangements to make. Calvain went south, into the narrow streets, and asked after the Lane of the Tanners.

He found Thessaly Morn in a small shop that smelled of hide and camphor, a woman perhaps sixty years of age with iron-gray hair cropped short and eyes the color of weak tea. She listened to his business without interruption, examined his pearls one by one through a jeweler's loupe of polished quartz, and named a price that was, as Dyron had said, perhaps a twelfth below what he might have had elsewhere. When he mentioned Dyron Faurd's name she looked up sharply, and for a moment something passed across her face that he could not read, something between surprise and a private grief.

"He is well?"

"He is well enough. He walked with me six days."

"Six days." She weighed this, as though it were a measure of something. "He does not usually walk with them so long. Tell me, did he tell you the story of the town of Ulm, and the man who went down into the cleft?"

"He did."

"And did he tell you whose story it was, originally?"

"He said it was his."

Thessaly Morn laughed, a short dry sound without much humor in it. She counted out his money in small bright coins of glass and bone.

"Take the third-day barge," she said. "Sit aft, not forward. Speak to no one. When you come to Erze Damath, go to the Inn of the Two Kestrels and ask for Beya. Tell her Thessaly sent you. She will put you on the river caravan to the coast, and from there, you will have choices I cannot make for you."

"Why are you helping me?"

She looked at him a long moment. "Because Dyron Faurd has not walked six days with anyone in nine years," she said, "and I find that I am curious to see what he has at last decided to walk away from. Go now. The barge leaves at dawn and Yan is not a town in which to sleep soundly."

Calvain went out into the late afternoon. The sun was sinking behind Cobalt Mountain and the white stucco of the town had gone the color of old amber. He found a small place on the south side of the square, as Dyron had advised, and ate a dish of stewed lentils without tasting it, and drank a cup of water from the clear fountain, and watched the shadows lengthen in the street.

At the next table sat a boy of perhaps eleven, alone, with a small dog curled at his feet, eating a bowl of the same stew with the slow concentration of the recently starved. Calvain watched him for some time. When the boy had finished, Calvain caught the eye of the woman who kept the place and paid for a second bowl to be brought, and a skin of water, and a small packet of dried meat for the road. The boy looked up once, startled, met Calvain's eyes across the room, and then looked down again and ate.

Six days to Erze Damath, Calvain thought. Then the river. Then the coast. Then he did not yet know.

But he found that he was hungry, for the first time in many weeks, and that the lentils were, after all, very good.
